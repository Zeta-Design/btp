# Real Estate Photography Pricing — Research Notes

> **Status: research only. Nothing here is committed or decided.**
> Input for a future pricing decision record (DR-0001) and Dubsado package setup.
> **Date:** 2026-06-11
> **The question:** What should a new real estate photographer charge in Central Florida (Orlando area)
> and Central Texas (Austin area) for residential, vacation-rental/STR, twilight, and drone work?
> **The constraint:** A solo photographer new to the market, targeting real estate agents and
> short-term rental (Airbnb/VRBO) hosts, including properties with themed rooms, game rooms, and
> other STR amenities. Goal is to set opening Dubsado packages that are competitive, cover real
> costs, and leave room to raise prices as the portfolio grows.

---

## 1. TL;DR

**The short version of what you actually need to know — no jargon.**

- **Standard shoot (25 photos, average home):** The market in both Orlando and Austin runs
  **$150–$250 for a small-to-average property, $250–$350 for larger homes**. Ben's current
  starting price of $175 for up to 25 photos sits at the *lower end* of the Central Florida market
  and is in the middle of the Austin entry range — it's not embarrassing, but there's room to
  charge more as a portfolio grows.
- **Vacation rental / Airbnb shoots:** These should cost *more* than a standard real estate shoot —
  typically **1.5–2× the standard rate** — because the photos stay up and generate income for
  years (not just until a house sells), and you're photographing an *experience*, not just a
  floor plan. Real Orlando competitors charge **$235–$295 for 1–4 bedroom STRs**; Ben's current
  $175 entry price is below market for this category.
- **Twilight / dusk shoots:** Typically a **separate appointment** (sunset timing is narrow, so it
  often can't be combined efficiently). Competitors charge **$145–$200 for a twilight add-on
  (5–8 photos)** or **$175–$350 as a standalone**. A virtual/digital twilight edit ($25–$50/image)
  is a cheaper alternative for clients who don't need real twilight.
- **Drone / aerial add-on:** **$80–$150 bundled with a photo shoot, $150–$250 standalone** for
  5–10 aerial stills. Requires an FAA Part 107 license to charge commercially — do not skip this.
- **The CODB rule (most important):** BLS data shows employed photographers earn a median of
  ~$42,500/year, but that's for W-2 employees at studios. Self-employed real estate photographers
  need to price above their *total costs* (gear, insurance, software, taxes, editing time, travel)
  before they make any profit. PPA's method: add up all your annual costs, add your desired salary,
  divide by realistic billable jobs per year — that's your minimum per-job price. Do this math
  *before* setting packages.
- **One thing to flag:** All rate figures below are from competitor price pages and community
  observations — they tell you what the *market accepts*, not whether those photographers are
  profitable. Run your own CODB calculation first.

## 2. The Reasoning

Real estate photography is a commodity market at the entry level — many photographers underprice
because they focus on what competitors charge rather than what they actually need to earn. The PPA
Cost of Doing Business method exists for exactly this reason: it anchors your floor price to *your*
costs, not the photographer down the street who might be losing money.

That said, knowing the market range is still essential — you can't charge $600 for a shoot that
the entire Orlando market delivers for $185. What follows is the competitive landscape for the two
markets in question.

**The Central Florida / Orlando market** has a well-established STR economy (Disney-area vacation
rentals are a major industry), so there are multiple competitors with public pricing. The range for
a standard residential shoot runs $155–$240 for smaller homes and up to $375–$595 for large
properties. Vacation rental pricing is explicitly higher at every competitor, ranging from $235 to
$295 for 1–4 bedroom properties — with add-ons like drone, twilight, and 360 tours pricing the
full package into the $400–$700 range for a well-equipped STR shoot.

**The Central Texas / Austin market** runs slightly lower on the entry end ($150–$195 for a basic
package) but scales up similarly. Square Foot Photography (a statewide Texas provider) charges
$185–$305 by square footage. A DFW provider (Future Media) charges $139–$269 for photos plus
photo+drone bundles at $259–$499.

**Vacation rental premium:** The 2× multiplier over standard real estate is a real-world
practitioner norm (documented by Aryeo's Matthew Digati and borne out in competitor pricing
pages). The reasoning is sound: a realtor's MLS photos are used once; an Airbnb host's photos are
a permanent marketing asset generating income for years. This also maps to Principle 2 (sell the
usage, not just the shoot). The Orlando competitor My Visual Listings builds this into explicit
separate pricing tiers: $155–$595 for resale vs. $235–$725 for vacation rentals.

**Themed rooms / game rooms / STR amenities:** No competitor publishes a line-item surcharge for
themed or game rooms specifically. However, this type of shoot takes longer because each themed
room needs its own stylistic treatment (the game room needs different angles and staging cues than
a bedroom), and the photos serve a commercial/advertising purpose long-term. This justifies
either a higher base STR rate or a per-room surcharge for properties with 3+ special-feature
rooms. No community or survey data exists specifically for this — it's a judgment call priced into
the base STR rate.

**Twilight:** A separate twilight session is almost always a separate appointment — it has to be,
because you're targeting a narrow 20-minute window at sunset. The standard community practice is
to price it at roughly the same rate as a standard photo package (so if you charge $200 for a
30-photo shoot, $200 for a twilight session is defensible), or as a flat add-on ($100–$200 extra).
Digital/virtual twilight edits are a lower-cost alternative ($25–$50/image) that satisfies most
MLS needs without a return trip.

**Drone:** Requires FAA Part 107. Add-on pricing runs $80–$150 when bundled with a photo shoot;
standalone starts around $150. This is a service worth adding as the portfolio grows — it's
increasingly expected in STR listings, especially for pool, amenity, and neighborhood shots.

**ASMP's licensing point:** For real estate agent use, the standard practice (documented in ASMP
guides) is a blanket MLS license included in the base price — images used for listing marketing.
For vacation rental clients, the license is effectively unlimited duration for that property's
marketing, which is why the higher rate is appropriate. At scale, a formal license agreement
(specifying use, media, duration, and property) is the professional standard.

**BLS baseline:** BLS OEWS data (May 2024) shows photographers in all settings earning a median
of ~$42,500/year. This is for employed photographers; it *excludes self-employed*. For a solo
real estate photographer working 40 weeks/year at 3 jobs/week = 120 jobs, charging $200/job
generates $24,000 — well below the BLS median. At $350/job × 120 jobs = $42,000, you're at the
median *before* expenses and self-employment taxes. This shows the math clearly: sub-$200 average
job prices require very high volume to yield a livable income.

## 3. Full Research

### 3.1 BLS Wage Data [Tier A]

**Source:** BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, Photographers (SOC 27-4021), May 2024 OEWS data.
URL: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/media-and-communication/photographers.htm
OEWS tables: https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes274021.htm

**Key data (May 2024):**
- **Median hourly wage:** $20.44/hr
- **Annualized (×2,080 hrs):** ~$42,515/yr
- **10th percentile:** <$14.23/hr (~$29,598/yr)
- **90th percentile:** >$45.56/hr (~$94,765/yr)
- **Employment outlook:** +2% growth 2024–2034 (slower than average)
- **Critical caveat:** OEWS data *excludes self-employed workers*. The majority of real estate
  photographers are self-employed or independent contractors. This figure represents W-2
  photographers at studios, newspapers, etc. — it is a floor reference, not a self-employment
  income target.

**Photographic Services industry (NAICS 541920):** BLS publishes a separate industry cut. The
2023 data shows photographers in the photographic services industry tend to earn slightly more than
the all-industries median, given the commercial/professional focus. Exact industry-specific
percentiles were not accessible directly (BLS pages returned 403 errors), but the OOH narrative
confirms real estate and commercial work pays above the median for the occupation.

**CODB math the BLS data supports:**
If a solo photographer wants to net $50,000/year (a modest self-employment goal), accounting for:
- Self-employment tax (~15.3% on net): adds ~$7,650 cost
- Business expenses (gear, software, insurance, marketing, travel, editing tools): conservatively
  $8,000–$15,000/year for a lean real estate setup
- Total needed gross: ~$65,000–$72,000/year

At 2 jobs/day, 3 days/week, 45 weeks/year = 270 jobs maximum (unrealistic at full capacity).
At a more realistic 150 completed jobs/year:
- $65,000 ÷ 150 = **$433/job minimum** to net $50K after taxes and expenses
- $72,000 ÷ 150 = **$480/job minimum**

At 200 jobs/year (busy solo):
- $65,000 ÷ 200 = **$325/job minimum**
- $72,000 ÷ 200 = **$360/job minimum**

This math shows why undercutting on price at $150–$175/job requires very high volume to be
viable — and why vacation rental shoots (which can be priced higher) improve the economics
significantly.

### 3.2 PPA Benchmark Data [Tier A — partially member-gated]

**Source:** Professional Photographers of America, Benchmark Survey + Square One Tool.
Primary URL: https://www.ppa.com/benchmark24
CODB methodology: https://www.ppa.com/articles/what-should-i-charge-photography-pricing-101
Profit article: https://www.ppa.com/articles/price-your-photography-for-a-profit

**What is publicly available:**
- PPA has run a Financial Benchmark Survey for 20+ years with results "consistent across all
  surveys." The detailed aggregate data (average studio revenue, margin breakdowns) is
  member-exclusive — not publicly quotable.
- **The one publicly confirmed benchmark:** PPA recommends cost of sales (CoS — what you spend
  directly to produce a job: second shooter, lab costs, props) should be no more than **25% of
  total revenue**. This is their anchor ratio from 20+ years of survey data.
- The "Square One Tool" walks photographers through a CODB calculation: enter your overhead
  (annual non-job expenses), desired salary, and expected number of shooting days → tool outputs
  the minimum you must charge per shooting day.
- The 2024 survey was completed; results are consistent with prior years per PPA's own statements.

**What this means practically:**
The PPA method is a framework, not a public rate sheet. The only public figure is the 25% CoS
ratio. For a real estate photographer whose direct job costs (driving, editing software, memory
cards) are low (unlike a portrait photographer buying lab prints), CoS tends to be closer to
10–15%, meaning overhead and salary consume a larger share of the fee — and overhead must be
covered by the base rate.

**Recommendation:** Use the PPA Square One Tool (free to try) or work the CODB math manually
(see Section 3.1) before setting Dubsado packages.

### 3.3 ASMP Guidance [Tier B]

**Sources:**
- ASMP Architectural Photography Best Practices PDF: https://www.asmp.org/wp-content/uploads/Architectural_Photo_BestPractices.pdf
- ASMP Licensing Guide (Jim Cavanaugh commercial example): https://www.asmp.org/professional-development/pricing-guides/licensing-guide-jim-cavanaugh-fees/
- ASMP "Establishing the Fee": https://www.asmp.org/strictly-business-blog/establishing-the-fee/
- ASMP Colorado Creative Fee detail: https://www.asmpcolorado.org/tutorials/details-creative-fee.html
- ASMP Multi-party licensing: https://www.asmp.org/paperwork-share-examples/architecture-multiple-parties/

**What ASMP provides and doesn't provide:**
ASMP does not publish a rate card. Their guidance is methodological and licensing-focused.

**Key ASMP principles for real estate / architectural photography:**

1. **Fee structure:** Three components — (a) creative/production fee, (b) direct expenses
   (mileage, assistants, etc.), (c) license fee. Real estate listing photography typically bundles
   (a) and (c) into one flat price with an implied MLS license.

2. **Standard architectural licensing practice:** "Unlimited use of images for a clearly defined
   blanket of marketing uses" is the long-established trade practice for architectural clients.
   This is what MLS/listing pricing typically delivers. However, ASMP documents a growing trend
   toward term-limited licenses or advertising licensing models for higher-value commercial work.

3. **Vacation rental distinction:** STR/Airbnb photography functions more like commercial
   advertising photography (ongoing income generation, multi-year use) than single-listing real
   estate. ASMP's commercial licensing framework applies: the license should be scoped by use,
   media, duration, and geography. In practice for a solo RE photographer, this means a higher
   base fee for STR work (which the market already accepts) with a simple written license clause
   in the contract.

4. **CODB formula (ASMP Colorado):**
   `(Non-reimbursable expenses + desired salary) ÷ billable days = CODB per day`
   Billable days for most photographers: 1–3 per week, accounting for marketing, admin,
   portfolio work, and non-billable time. At 2 billable days/week × 48 weeks = 96 billable days.

5. **Creative fee additions:** ASMP says charge above CODB for tight deadlines, specialized skill,
   geographic challenges, logistical complexity, and reputation. All of these apply as a
   portfolio grows and the photographer gains a body of work.

**Jim Cavanaugh's commercial example (ASMP):**
This is a full commercial architectural assignment (design-firm client, multiple parties). His
numbers aren't directly comparable to residential real estate pricing, but show the structure:
- Assistant: $200/day (8 hrs), $37.50/hr overtime
- Post-production: $60/RAW file conversion + $125/hr retouching ($750 average)
- Mileage: $0.50/mile beyond first 100 miles
- Out-of-town expenses: cost + 25%

**ASMP's key legal point for real estate photographers:**
Copyright in the photos belongs to the photographer at the moment of capture — not to the real
estate agent or the homeowner. The license (explicit or implied) is what the client is buying.
For STR clients who will use images for years, having a short written license statement in the
contract (even one sentence) is professional best practice and protects both parties.

> **Legal/tax line:** Drafting an enforceable license agreement or work-for-hire clause requires
> a qualified IP attorney. Cite the US Copyright Office (https://www.copyright.gov/registration/
> photographs/) and ASMP resources as starting points; get a lawyer to review any contract before
> relying on it commercially.

### 3.9 Evaluation Against the Principle Lens

**Principle 1 (Price from CODB, not competitors):**
The market data (Sections 3.4–3.8) establishes what the market will bear. But this must be
anchored to a personal CODB calculation first. Ben's current $175 entry for a residential shoot
*might* cover costs if overhead is very low (home-based, low gear payment, minimal insurance) and
volume is high — but the Section 3.1 math shows it likely doesn't produce a living wage at
realistic job volumes without a lot of bookings. **Recommendation: Run the CODB before finalizing
packages.**

**Principle 2 (Sell the value and usage, not the hours):**
STR/Airbnb shoots are a direct application of this — they justify 1.5–2× pricing because the
usage is perpetual and commercial, not one-time listing documentation. The vacation rental market
in both Orlando (Disney-area STR economy) and Austin (major STR market) makes this premium
achievable. The 2× rule is well-documented in practitioner sources.

**Principle 3 (Know and protect the rights):**
Copyright belongs to the photographer. For MLS use, an implied license is industry standard
(ASMP confirms). For STR/Airbnb clients, a written license clause (even a single sentence:
"License granted for use in marketing [property address] on short-term rental platforms for the
duration of ownership or management") protects both parties. **Do not skip this for STR clients.**
> Legal line: Have a lawyer review any license language before relying on it.

**Principle 4 (Nothing happens without a signed contract and deposit):**
Directly applicable. Dubsado should have: signed contract before shoot, deposit (50% or full
payment for smaller jobs) at booking, remainder due at delivery. This is especially important for
STR clients who may be remote/out-of-state property owners.

**Principle 5 (Client experience is the product):**
24-hour turnaround is table stakes in both markets (every competitor offers it). Going to 48 hours
is competitively acceptable but positions you below the premium tier. Delivering a short selection
of social-media-formatted versions alongside MLS photos is a differentiator at low marginal cost.

**Principle 6 (Specialize to escape price competition):**
STR / vacation rental + themed amenity rooms is a genuine niche in Orlando (Disney-area properties)
and Austin (music/event tourism STR market). Positioning explicitly as the "vacation rental
specialist" rather than a general real estate photographer justifies the higher STR rate and
attracts the clients who value that expertise. This is the most important strategic decision for
this business.

**Principle 8 (Systematize the repeatable):**
Dubsado packages should encode the pricing and license tiers discovered in this research so
decisions aren't remade per job. Create templates for: (1) standard residential, (2) STR/vacation
rental, (3) twilight add-on, (4) drone add-on.

### 3.10 Recommendation

**What to actually put in Dubsado:**

*These are suggested ranges informed by the research above. Final prices must pass the personal
CODB test — do not copy these without running your own numbers first.*

**Package 1: Standard Residential (MLS)**
- 20–30 photos, interior + exterior, 24-hr delivery, MLS license
- Pricing signal: $175–$235 (Ben's current $175 is at market floor; $195–$225 is more defensible
  once a portfolio exists)
- Tier by square footage if preferred (as most competitors do): <2,000 sq ft / 2,000–4,000 / 4,000+

**Package 2: Vacation Rental / Short-Term Rental**
- 25–40 photos, interior + exterior + amenity/lifestyle details, 24–48 hr delivery, extended
  STR marketing license (include a written clause), formatted for Airbnb/VRBO platforms
- Pricing signal: $235–$295 for 1–4 bedroom properties (Orlando benchmark from My Visual Listings),
  $210–$295 for Austin/Texas (current market)
- For properties with themed rooms or game rooms: treat as a premium STR tier or add a per-room
  surcharge ($25–$50/special feature room) — there is no published market rate for this; use
  judgment based on time added

**Package 3: Drone Add-On (requires Part 107)**
- 5–10 aerial stills, bundled with photo shoot
- Pricing signal: $80–$130 (add-on), $150–$250 (standalone)
- Do not offer this service without the FAA Part 107 certificate

**Package 4: Twilight / Dusk Add-On**
- Option A — Virtual twilight: $25–$50/image (post-production, same-day delivery)
- Option B — Real twilight session: $145–$200 as an add-on (separate appointment)
- For a new photographer, virtual twilight is lower-friction and market-accepted for most MLS use

**Package 5: Walkthrough / Social Video Add-On**
- 60–90 sec highlight video, 24–48 hr delivery
- Pricing signal: $99–$175 as an add-on (Future Media DFW, Real Estate Photography FL)
- Ben's current $150 add-on price is well within market range

**Vacation Rental Premium Summary:**
Current Ben pricing ($175 for 25 photos) is below the STR market rate in both Orlando ($235–$295)
and Austin ($210–$260). The research strongly supports either creating an explicit STR package
at $235–$275 or applying the 1.5× multiplier to the base rate. At current base rates, a $250–$275
STR package is conservative and market-supported.
